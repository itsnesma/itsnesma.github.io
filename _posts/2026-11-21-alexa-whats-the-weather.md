Can Alexa predict the next thunderstorm and save us from the next global crisis? Probably not, but you should definitely ask her if you’re going to need a jacket for brunch on Saturday morning.
With a wake word, you can explore the complexity of interacting with a device such as the Amazon Echo and allowing it to influence your daily decisions and assist you in menial tasks. This is a small scale example of the power of data and machine learning.

A much broader application of machine learning is the use of algorithms to detect shape and motion of clouds to forecast severe thunderstorms and cyclones that may have fatal impact on a national, and even global level.
The study done by Zheng et al (2019) shows how matching existing data on storm detection with annotated comma-shaped cloud datasets can aid meteorologists in weather forecasting. The comma-shaped cloud is associated with many types of cyclones, hail, thunderstorm, high winds, blizzards, and low-pressure systems.

The purpose of investing billions in modern weather research is due to attempting to alleviate the consequences of extreme weather, allowing for better preparation in advance and preventing the loss of human life. Investing in high quality and high definition cameras as well as updated satellites has produced impeccable images that can be used in the automated process of shape and motion detection.

Employing experts in cloud formation and using conventional ways of detecting storm formation is not feasible anymore as the weather has been more and more dynamic with the phenomenon of climate change. Extreme weather conditions are a pressing and urgent matter and improving the machinery and processing of satellite images at a fast and reliable pace can prevent further damage.

Zheng et al (2019) proposed machine learning and pattern recognition based approach to detect comma-shaped clouds from satellite images. The visual cues that experts utilise in distinguishing comma-shaped clouds are shape and motion. When a cyclone forms, a rotation feature can be seen in the head of the cloud and due to its density it will form the imbalanced proportion of a comma shape.

Two novel features that consider both shape and motion of the cloud patches that the team used were 1) segmented histogram of oriented gradient (HOG) and 2) motion correlation histogram. These two techniques enable the detection of the comma shape from HD satellite images through cloud segmentation and motion-prior mapping.

Using increasing amounts of HD images and consistent data, machine learning will encompass the high variability of cloud appearance in satellite images affected by seasonal, geographical and temporal factors and over time will improve in robustness and reliability. Anomalies could also be tracked and fed into the pool of data, potentially, anomalies that would otherwise be overlooked especially if they aren’t central to the investigation.
As a conclusion, machine learning is the future of time-sensitive severe weather forecasting and these aforementioned techniques which Zheng et al (2019) explained in their paper demonstrate success and positive feedback.
